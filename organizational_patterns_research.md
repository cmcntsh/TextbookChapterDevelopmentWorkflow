# Authoritative and Scholarly Sources on Organizational Patterns in Writing

There is a substantial research base behind common organizational strategies, but one qualification is important: **the evidence does not show that one pattern is intrinsically superior across all writing tasks**. The strongest finding is that readers benefit when the organizational pattern matches the conceptual relationships in the material and when that structure is made perceptible through headings, previews, sequencing, and other rhetorical signals.

Experimental work is particularly strong for **comparison, problem–solution, causal, hierarchical/topical, and sequential structures**.

Your examples also combine two patterns that are normally treated separately in rhetoric: **climactic/order-of-importance** organization arranges ideas by rhetorical importance or force, whereas **spatial** organization arranges them according to physical or conceptual location.

---

## 1. Meyer & Freedle: the strongest direct experimental comparison of common patterns

This is probably the single most relevant peer-reviewed article for this question. Meyer and Freedle experimentally compared four discourse structures containing essentially comparable information:

- **comparison**
- **problem–solution**
- **causation**
- **collection of descriptions**, roughly analogous to a loose topical/list structure

The two studies found better recall for the more explicitly relational structures—comparison, problem–solution, and causation—than for information presented as a collection of descriptions.

Their explanation is not that writers should always choose one of those three patterns, but that structures encoding clear relationships among ideas give readers a stronger retrieval framework.

### Practical selection principle

Use a structure that expresses the **strongest genuine relationship among the ideas**:

| If the underlying relationship is... | A strong candidate is... |
|---|---|
| one condition produces another | Cause–effect |
| an undesirable condition calls for remedy | Problem–solution |
| two or more alternatives share dimensions | Comparison/contrast |
| material consists primarily of related categories | Topical/hierarchical |

### APA citation

Meyer, B. J. F., & Freedle, R. O. (1984). Effects of discourse type on recall. *American Educational Research Journal, 21*(1), 121–143. https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312021001121

## 2. Meyer: foundational theory of “top-level” text structures

Meyer's monograph is foundational to modern research on expository text structure. It analyzes prose as a hierarchy in which propositions are connected by rhetorical relations such as causation, comparison, description, and problem–solution.

Its major lesson for writers is that organization should not be viewed merely as **ordering sentences**. Effective prose has a **top-level structure** that tells readers how major units of information relate.

### Practical selection principle

Before outlining, complete this sentence:

> “The major ideas in this material relate to one another primarily as ______.”

Possible answers include:

- stages in time,
- causes and consequences,
- problem and response,
- similarities and differences,
- classes and subclasses,
- components of a system.

The answer gives you a defensible starting organizational pattern.

### APA citation

Meyer, B. J. F. (1975). *The organization of prose and its effects on memory*. North-Holland. ISBN 978-0-444-10946-0. OCLC 1530731.

## 3. Meyer, Brandt, & Bluth: recognizing structure predicts comprehension

This peer-reviewed study investigated ninth-grade readers' use of the **top-level structure** of expository texts. The research helped establish that readers who identify and use a text's organizational structure are better able to comprehend and reconstruct its important information.

This finding is highly relevant to writers: organization is most useful when the reader can **recognize the organizing relationship**.

### Practical selection principle

Do not merely *have* an organizational pattern. **Signal it**.

For example:

> Three factors account for the decline...

signals classification/topical structure.

> The failure resulted from three interacting conditions...

signals causation.

> Two approaches solve the same problem in fundamentally different ways...

signals comparison.

### APA citation

Meyer, B. J. F., Brandt, D. M., & Bluth, G. J. (1980). Use of top-level structure in text: Key for reading comprehension of ninth-grade students. *Reading Research Quarterly, 16*(1), 72–103. https://doi.org/10.2307/747349

## 4. Kintsch & van Dijk: organize according to local and global meaning

Kintsch and van Dijk's landmark model distinguishes between the **microstructure** of a text—local relationships among propositions—and its **macrostructure**, the larger conceptual organization or gist.

The implication for writing is important: organization operates at multiple levels.

A document can have:

- paragraphs that are individually coherent,
- yet a poorly organized overall argument;

or:

- a sound high-level outline,
- yet sentences that do not connect locally.

Good organization requires both.

### Practical selection principle

Choose the **global pattern first**, then organize smaller units recursively.

For example:

**Document level:** problem → causes → solutions

**Within causes section:**

- cause 1 → mechanism → evidence
- cause 2 → mechanism → evidence
- cause 3 → mechanism → evidence

### APA citation

Kintsch, W., & van Dijk, T. A. (1978). Toward a model of text comprehension and production. *Psychological Review, 85*(5), 363–394. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.85.5.363

## 5. Lorch & Lorch: complex structures should be explicitly signaled

This peer-reviewed experimental study examined headings, overviews, and summaries as **organizational signals**. When text organization was relatively complex, signaling affected readers' recall and the way they organized remembered information. When structure was simple, signals provided less benefit.

This gives writers an important conditional rule:

> **The more complex the structure, the more explicitly you should expose that structure to the reader.**

### Practical applications

For a simple chronological account, paragraph order may be self-evident.

For a complex report containing causes, alternatives, trade-offs, and recommendations, use:

- descriptive headings,
- previews,
- explicit transition sentences,
- summaries,
- parallel section structures.

### APA citation

Lorch, R. F., Jr., & Lorch, E. P. (1996). Effects of organizational signals on free recall of expository text. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 88*(1), 38–48. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.38

## 6. Lorch, Lemarié, & Grant: hierarchical versus sequential organization

This four-experiment peer-reviewed study is particularly useful for deciding between **topical/hierarchical** and **sequential** organization.

The researchers found that headings communicating hierarchical organization helped readers construct more accurate outlines, whereas headings signaling sequential organization helped readers search sequentially organized information more efficiently.

Different structures therefore serve different cognitive tasks.

### When to choose each

**Hierarchical/topical organization** is especially appropriate when readers need to understand:

> whole → categories → subcategories

Examples:

- dimensions of a strategy,
- components of a system,
- categories of risk,
- branches of a theory.

**Sequential organization** is especially appropriate when readers need to understand:

> stage 1 → stage 2 → stage 3

Examples:

- procedures,
- workflows,
- historical development,
- causal chains with temporal stages.

### APA citation

Lorch, R., Lemarié, J., & Grant, R. (2011). Signaling hierarchical and sequential organization in expository text. *Scientific Studies of Reading, 15*(3), 267–284. https://doi.org/10.1080/10888431003747535

## 7. Lorch: review of the evidence on organizational signaling

Lorch's scholarly review synthesizes research on devices that tell readers how a text is organized, including headings, previews, summaries, and other cues.

This source is useful because selecting a good pattern is only half the job. Writers also need to ensure readers can **detect the pattern**.

### Practical selection principle

Once you decide on the pattern, ask:

> What linguistic or visual cues will tell a reader what this structure is?

For example:

**Chronological**
- initially
- subsequently
- after
- finally

**Causal**
- because
- results from
- therefore
- consequently

**Problem–solution**
- problem
- obstacle
- alternative
- remedy
- recommendation

**Comparative**
- similarly
- whereas
- by contrast
- unlike

### APA citation

Lorch, R. F., Jr. (1989). Text-signaling devices and their effects on reading and memory processes. *Educational Psychology Review, 1*, 209–234. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320135

## 8. Swales & Feak: choose structure according to communicative purpose and genre

This is one of the strongest authoritative sources for academic and professional writing. Swales and Feak explicitly teach writers to consider **audience, purpose, genre, flow of ideas, general-to-specific development, problem–solution structures, process description, data commentary, critique, and research-article organization**.

Their contribution goes beyond recognizing patterns. It provides a selection principle:

> **Organization should follow the communicative task the writer is performing.**

### Examples

If the purpose is to...

**Explain a process**  
→ chronological/sequential

**Explain why something occurred**  
→ cause–effect

**Diagnose and recommend**  
→ problem → causes → alternatives → recommendation

**Define a domain**  
→ general → specific or topical classification

**Position new research**  
→ established territory → gap/problem → present contribution

### APA citation

Swales, J. M., & Feak, C. B. (2012). *Academic writing for graduate students: Essential tasks and skills* (3rd ed.). University of Michigan Press. https://doi.org/10.3998/mpub.2173936

ISBN 978-0-472-03475-8 (paperback); ISBN 978-0-472-12848-8 (ebook).

## 9. O'Hair, Rubenstein, & Stewart: a clear authoritative taxonomy of common patterns

This university public-speaking text devotes an entire chapter to **selecting an organizational pattern**. Its coverage includes:

- chronological,
- spatial,
- causal,
- problem–solution,
- topical,
- narrative.

It additionally emphasizes purpose statements, main-point unity, coordination/subordination, coherence, transitions, and audience adaptation.

This is one of the most useful references if your goal is to document the **standard rhetorical taxonomy** used in contemporary communication instruction.

### Selection framework

Use:

| Pattern | Most natural when the controlling relationship is... |
|---|---|
| Chronological | time or stages |
| Sequential | procedural dependency |
| Spatial | physical or geographic relationship |
| Causal | causes and consequences |
| Problem–solution | condition requiring remedy |
| Topical | conceptual categories |
| Narrative | events whose meaning emerges through a story |

### APA citation

O'Hair, D., Rubenstein, H., & Stewart, R. (2019). *A pocket guide to public speaking* (6th ed.). Bedford/St. Martin's. ISBN 978-1-319-10278-4.

Electronic ISBN: 978-1-319-21709-9.

## 10. Keith & Lundberg: contemporary authoritative treatment of speech organization

Keith and Lundberg's public-speaking textbook has a dedicated organization chapter covering:

- chronological organization,
- spatial organization,
- cause and effect,
- problem–solution,
- topical organization,
- Monroe's Motivated Sequence,
- combinations of patterns,
- primacy versus recency in point ordering.

Its inclusion of **combination structures** is particularly important: sophisticated writing rarely has to use one pattern exclusively.

### Practical selection principle

You can nest structures.

For example:

**Problem–solution at the document level**

1. Problem
2. Causes
3. Solutions

Then inside the solution section:

**Comparison structure**

- Alternative A
- Alternative B
- Alternative C

Then inside each alternative:

**Topical structure**

- cost
- feasibility
- benefits
- risks

This nested approach is often substantially more useful than asking which *single* organizational pattern the whole document must use.

### APA citation

Keith, W., & Lundberg, C. O. (2024). *Public speaking: Choices and responsibility* (4th ed.). Cengage. ISBN 978-0-357-79892-8.

Electronic ISBN: 978-0-357-79903-1.

## 11. Monroe/Ehninger: a specialized persuasive sequence

For persuasive writing and speaking, **Monroe's Motivated Sequence** is an important extension of simple problem–solution organization:

1. attention
2. need
3. satisfaction
4. visualization
5. action

It was developed by Alan H. Monroe and became a canonical pattern in speech communication.

It is particularly appropriate when the rhetorical goal is not merely to explain a solution but to move an audience toward a **specific action**.

### Best fit

Instead of:

> problem → solution

use:

> attention → demonstrate need → propose remedy → show consequences → request action

when behavioral persuasion is the objective.

### APA citation

Ehninger, D. (Ed.). (1986). *Principles and types of speech communication* (10th ed.). Scott, Foresman. ISBN 978-0-673-18156-5.

The book retains the framework originally developed by Alan H. Monroe.

## 12. Haugtvedt & Wegener: evidence on “strongest first” versus “strongest last”

This research is particularly relevant to what is often called the **climactic pattern**.

It would be too strong to say that research proves writers should always put the strongest material last. Persuasion research finds both **primacy effects** and **recency effects**, depending on how readers process the information.

Haugtvedt and Wegener found that conditions producing more extensive message elaboration increased the influence of the **earlier** message, whereas lower levels of message-relevant elaboration increased the relative influence of the **later** message.

That is an important correction to simplistic writing advice.

### Better selection rule

Do **not** assume:

> least important → most important

is invariably best.

Instead consider:

- **Primacy** when the first argument establishes the interpretive frame or readers will engage deeply with it.
- **Recency/climactic order** when you want the argument immediately preceding the conclusion or decision point to remain especially accessible.
- **Strong–weak–strong** or other mixed arrangements when several persuasive constraints must be managed.

### APA citation

Haugtvedt, C. P., & Wegener, D. T. (1994). Message order effects in persuasion: An attitude strength perspective. *Journal of Consumer Research, 21*(1), 205–218. https://doi.org/10.1086/209393

---

## 13. Crano: additional experimental evidence on primacy and recency

Crano experimentally investigated the order of opposing persuasive communications and found that order effects depended on conditions affecting attention and retention.

This is further evidence against treating climactic ordering as a universal rhetorical law.

### APA citation

Crano, W. D. (1977). Primacy versus recency in retention of information and opinion change. *The Journal of Social Psychology, 101*(1), 87–96. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224545.1977.9923987

# How the research maps onto common organizational patterns

| Pattern | Controlling relationship | Especially appropriate for | Research/theoretical support |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chronological** | A happened before B | history, development, biographies | Lorch et al.; O'Hair et al. |
| **Sequential/procedural** | B cannot occur until A is completed | instructions, workflows, processes | Lorch et al.; O'Hair et al. |
| **Problem–solution** | an undesirable condition requires a remedy | recommendations, proposals, persuasive writing | Meyer & Freedle; Swales & Feak |
| **Problem–cause–solution** | understanding causes is necessary before choosing remedy | policy/technical analysis | public-speaking/rhetorical literature |
| **Cause–effect** | X produces/contributes to Y | scientific explanation, diagnosis, analysis | Meyer & Freedle |
| **Comparison–contrast** | alternatives share comparable dimensions | evaluation, selection, synthesis | Meyer & Freedle |
| **Topical/categorical** | ideas belong to distinguishable classes | surveys, overviews, conceptual explanations | Meyer; Lorch et al. |
| **Hierarchical** | categories contain subordinate categories | technical reports, taxonomy, complex exposition | Meyer; Lorch et al. |
| **Spatial** | relationships depend on physical/geographic arrangement | anatomy, architecture, geography, system layout | O'Hair et al.; Keith & Lundberg |
| **General → specific** | broad principle contains narrower cases | definitions, academic exposition | Swales & Feak |
| **Specific → general** | examples support an inferred generalization | inductive explanation, synthesis | Swales & Feak |
| **Climactic / importance** | ideas differ in rhetorical importance | persuasive emphasis, crescendo | rhetoric texts; persuasion-order studies require qualification |
| **Monroe's Motivated Sequence** | audience moves psychologically from need to action | advocacy and calls to action | Monroe/Ehninger |
| **Narrative** | significance emerges through events and change | case studies, experience, illustrative persuasion | O'Hair et al. |

# A research-grounded method for choosing the pattern

Across these sources, a reasonably defensible selection procedure emerges.

## 1. Start with the relationship inherent in the material

Do not begin by picking a favorite outline.

Ask:

**What actually connects these ideas?**

If the answer is:

- time → **chronological**
- procedural dependency → **sequential**
- physical position → **spatial**
- membership in categories → **topical/hierarchical**
- causal dependence → **cause–effect**
- competing alternatives → **comparison**
- deficiency and remedy → **problem–solution**

This is the central lesson of Meyer and the broader text-structure literature.

## 2. Then identify the reader's task

Ask what the reader needs to do with the information.

If they need to...

**reproduce a process**  
→ sequential.

**understand why something happened**  
→ causal.

**choose among options**  
→ comparison organized around common criteria.

**understand a complex field**  
→ hierarchical/topical.

**approve a recommendation**  
→ problem → causes/constraints → alternatives → recommendation.

**take action**  
→ problem–solution or a motivational sequence.

Lorch et al.'s experiments are especially useful here because they show that **hierarchical and sequential structures support different information-processing tasks**.

## 3. Use the most informative relationship rather than a generic topical list

A generic topical organization is useful when the subject genuinely consists of categories. But where stronger relationships exist—causality, comparison, or problem–solution—the experimental literature gives good reason to expose those relationships instead of reducing everything to a list.

For example, this:

> 1. Labor costs  
> 2. Automation  
> 3. Productivity  
> 4. Pricing

may be less informative than:

> Rising labor costs → investment in automation → productivity gains → effects on pricing

if those variables actually form a causal chain.

## 4. Distinguish chronological order from causal order

These are often confused.

> A happened, then B happened

is **chronological**.

> A contributed to B

is **causal**.

Temporal succession by itself does not establish causation.

Use causal organization only when evidence supports the relationship.

## 5. Distinguish spatial order from climactic order

These should not be combined into one category.

**Spatial:**

> north → central → south  
> exterior → interior  
> left → right

**Climactic/order of importance:**

> minor consideration → important consideration → decisive consideration

Traditional rhetoric recognizes both, but they perform fundamentally different functions.

## 6. Treat “strongest last” as a strategy, not a law

The persuasion literature does **not** establish a universal recency advantage. Depending on elaboration, attention, timing, and task, either earlier or later material can exert disproportionate influence.

So the more defensible rule is:

> **Sequence important points according to how the reader will process and use them, rather than automatically saving the strongest point for last.**

## 7. Signal the organization explicitly when complexity rises

Once you select the pattern, reveal it using:

- headings,
- preview sentences,
- parallel wording,
- numbered stages,
- transition sentences,
- summaries.

Experimental evidence shows these signals become particularly valuable when readers face complex topic structures.

## 8. Combine patterns when the rhetorical problem requires it

Real professional documents often use **nested organization**.

For example:

> **I. Problem**  
> **II. Causes** — causal structure  
> **III. Solutions** — topical alternatives  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A. Solution A  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;B. Solution B  
> **IV. Comparison of solutions** — comparison structure  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;cost  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;effectiveness  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;risk  
> **V. Recommendation**

There is no theoretical reason to force the entire document into one pattern. Contemporary rhetorical texts explicitly recognize combinations, and hierarchical discourse models naturally permit structures to be nested.

# The strongest general rule

If the scholarship is reduced to one usable decision rule, it is:

> **Choose the organizational pattern that makes the most important relationship among the ideas explicit for the task the reader needs to perform; then signal that relationship strongly enough that the reader does not have to reconstruct the organization unaided.**

That formulation is more consistent with the empirical literature than rules such as “always use chronological order for processes,” “always put the strongest argument last,” or “problem–solution is always best for persuasion.”

For a compact scholarly bibliography, the strongest starting points are:

- Meyer (1975)
- Meyer, Brandt, and Bluth (1980)
- Meyer and Freedle (1984)
- Kintsch and van Dijk (1978)
- Lorch and Lorch (1996)
- Lorch, Lemarié, and Grant (2011)
- Swales and Feak (2012)
- Haugtvedt and Wegener (1994)

Together, these sources provide empirical evidence, discourse theory, academic-writing practice, and evidence about ordering effects.
