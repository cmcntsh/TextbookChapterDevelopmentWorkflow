# Authoritative and Scholarly Sources on Organizing Information into Paragraphs and Sequencing Paragraphs

The scholarship does not support a single universally “best” paragraph pattern or paragraph sequence. The strongest recurring principle is **functional organization**: choose a structure that makes the relationships among ideas apparent to the reader and that fits the writer’s purpose, genre, audience, and subject matter. Research on comprehension additionally suggests that recognizable relational structures—such as **comparison, causation, and problem–solution**—can improve readers’ comprehension and recall compared with a relatively unstructured collection of facts.

Below are the sources I would recommend most strongly. I have separated foundational rhetoric/composition scholarship from empirical discourse/comprehension research.

## 1. Rodgers: the paragraph as a unit of discourse, not a formula

Rodgers's article is one of the classic challenges to the traditional notion that every paragraph should simply be an “expanded sentence” consisting of a topic sentence plus supporting details. He treats paragraphing as a **discourse-level rhetorical decision**. This is particularly useful for deciding **where paragraph boundaries belong and how paragraphs should progress**, rather than mechanically imposing a template.

A major implication is that paragraph structure should emerge from the writer's developing discourse: group material according to what the passage is accomplishing, and begin a new paragraph when the discourse makes a meaningful rhetorical shift.

**APA citation**

Rodgers, P. C., Jr. (1966). A discourse-centered rhetoric of the paragraph. *College Composition and Communication, 17*(1), 2–11. https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc196621005

---

## 2. Fahnestock: organize paragraphs around semantic relationships

Fahnestock's article is especially close to your question. She examines **coherence within paragraphs**, including the traditional idea that writers create coherence by choosing a “model of development.” Her treatment moves beyond merely inserting transitional expressions: coherence depends substantially on the **semantic and lexical relationships among ideas**.

For practical purposes, this means deciding first what relationship governs the material:

- cause → consequence
- generalization → example
- claim → evidence
- problem → solution
- whole → parts
- contrast between alternatives

Sentence order should then make that relationship progressively intelligible.

**APA citation**

Fahnestock, J. (1983). Semantic and lexical coherence. *College Composition and Communication, 34*(4), 400–416. https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc198315260

The original JSTOR DOI is also `10.2307/357897`.

---

## 3. Witte & Faigley: cohesion is not the same thing as coherence

This is another foundational composition article. Witte and Faigley distinguish **surface cohesion**—explicit linguistic connections among sentences—from the larger phenomenon of **coherence**. Their work is important because writers sometimes try to repair bad organization merely by adding transitions such as *however*, *therefore*, and *in addition*. That cannot substitute for an intelligible conceptual sequence.

The practical implication is:

> First establish a logical relationship among propositions and paragraphs; then use linguistic signals to make that relationship easier to perceive.

**APA citation**

Witte, S. P., & Faigley, L. (1981). Coherence, cohesion, and writing quality. *College Composition and Communication, 32*(2), 189–204. https://doi.org/10.58680/ccc198115912

---

## 4. Mann & Thompson: choose organization according to rhetorical relations

**Rhetorical Structure Theory (RST)** provides perhaps the most systematic theoretical answer to the question “What relationship should come next?” Mann and Thompson argue that coherent texts are built from functional relationships among spans of discourse rather than simply from a sequence of topics.

Among the relationships RST can represent are functions roughly corresponding to:

- evidence
- elaboration
- justification
- cause/result
- condition
- contrast
- concession
- background
- motivation
- sequence

This produces a powerful planning question for every paragraph:

**What rhetorical relationship does this paragraph bear to the preceding material and to the central claim?**

That is usually more productive than asking, “What paragraph should come second?”

**APA citation**

Mann, W. C., & Thompson, S. A. (1988). Rhetorical Structure Theory: Toward a functional theory of text organization. *Text, 8*(3), 243–281. https://doi.org/10.1515/text.1.1988.8.3.243

An expanded technical-report version is:

Mann, W. C., & Thompson, S. A. (1987). *Rhetorical Structure Theory: A theory of text organization* (ISI/RS-87-190; NTIS No. ADA 183038). Information Sciences Institute, University of Southern California. https://www.sfu.ca/rst/05bibliographies/access.html

## 5. Meyer & Freedle: empirical evidence for choosing among expository structures

This is one of the most directly relevant empirical studies. Meyer and Freedle compared four ways of organizing substantially the same information:

- comparison
- problem/solution
- causation
- collection/descriptive listing

Their experiments found better recall for the more explicitly structured **comparison, problem/solution, and causation** organizations than for a looser collection of descriptions.

That gives a useful decision rule: **when the content permits a meaningful causal, comparative, or problem–solution structure, prefer that relationship to an arbitrary list of facts.**

It does *not* mean one of these structures is always superior; appropriateness depends upon what relationship actually exists among the ideas.

**APA citation**

Meyer, B. J. F., & Freedle, R. O. (1984). Effects of discourse type on recall. *American Educational Research Journal, 21*(1), 121–143. https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312021001121

The article also circulated under the JSTOR DOI `10.2307/1162357`.

---

## 6. Goldman, Saul, & Coté: paragraphing itself affects comprehension

This experimental work investigates paragraphing as a reader cue, specifically examining its effects on readers' identification and memory of **main ideas versus elaborative information**.

It supports an important rhetorical principle: paragraph boundaries are not merely visual decoration. They **signal conceptual grouping and hierarchy**. A writer should therefore place breaks where readers ought to perceive a shift in the discourse unit.

**APA citation**

Goldman, S. R., Saul, E. U., & Coté, N. (1995). Paragraphing, reader, and task effects on discourse comprehension. *Discourse Processes, 20*(3), 273–305. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539509544942

---

## 7. Kent: the given–new principle

Kent applies the **given–new contract** to paragraph production. The basic information-flow principle is that readers process prose more easily when writers establish connections to information already active in the reader's mind before introducing unfamiliar information.

This suggests a highly practical sequencing principle at both sentence and paragraph level:

**Given / familiar / contextual information → new / important / consequential information.**

The new information at one point can then become the given information from which the next unit proceeds.

**APA citation**

Kent, T. L. (1984). Paragraph production and the given-new contract. *The Journal of Business Communication, 21*(4), 45–54. https://doi.org/10.1177/002194368402100405

---

## 8. Weissberg: given–new progression in authentic scientific prose

Weissberg examined published scientific English and found limitations in teaching paragraphs merely through fixed traditional categories such as “definition,” “cause/effect,” and “comparison/contrast.” His analysis emphasizes **given–new information development** instead.

This is valuable because it shows that actual professional paragraphs often develop through **information flow** rather than by perfectly instantiating textbook paragraph templates.

**APA citation**

Weissberg, R. C. (1984). Given and new: Paragraph development models from scientific English. *TESOL Quarterly, 18*(3), 485–500. https://doi.org/10.2307/3586716

---

## 9. McGee: a modern critique of rigid paragraph formulas

McGee synthesizes research from linguistics, psychology, education, and discourse studies and explicitly argues against both completely laissez-faire paragraphing and excessively prescriptive formulas. He proposes a **descriptivist pedagogy** based on how effective paragraphing actually works.

This is particularly useful if you are considering formulas such as PEEL, TEEL, PIE, or “topic sentence + three supports.” McGee provides scholarly grounds for treating such formulas as **training heuristics rather than universal rules**.

**APA citation**

McGee, I. (2016). Reconsidering paragraphing pedagogy: A descriptivist perspective. *English in Education, 50*(3), 233–254. https://doi.org/10.1111/eie.12112

---

## 10. McGee: argumentative paragraphing and rhetorical movement

McGee's earlier empirical/discourse study examines English argumentative texts and investigates the relationship among paragraph boundaries, **lexical cohesion, genre moves, and rhetorical features**.

It is useful for your question because it reinforces the idea that paragraphs should correspond not merely to “topics” but to **moves in an argument**.

A sequence might therefore be planned as:

claim → grounds → complication → response → implication,

rather than simply:

topic A → topic B → topic C.

**APA citation**

McGee, I. (2014). The pragmatics of paragraphing English argumentative text. *Journal of Pragmatics, 68*, 40–72. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2014.04.004

---

## 11. Williams & Bizup: one of the strongest practical treatments of coherence

*Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* is not a peer-reviewed journal article, but it is a highly established rhetoric/style text and is unusually useful for applying coherence theory to actual prose. The current edition explicitly treats **cohesion and coherence**, document framing, and section framing.

Its reader-centered approach strongly supports several useful organizational principles:

- establish familiar/contextual information before unfamiliar material;
- put important information where readers perceive emphasis;
- make the subjects of successive sentences form a coherent thematic sequence;
- frame sections so readers know what organizational purpose they serve;
- build global coherence, not merely local transitions.

**APA citation**

Williams, J. M., & Bizup, J. (2022). *Style: Lessons in clarity and grace* (13th ed.). Pearson. ISBN 978-0-13-517184-4.

An electronic edition is ISBN 978-0-13-753660-3.

---

## 12. Booth et al.: choose organization from the logic of the argument

*The Craft of Research* provides one of the clearest practical treatments of **whole-document organization**. Its fourth edition contains separate chapters on assembling reasons and evidence, acknowledgments and responses, warrants, planning, **organizing an argument**, and introductions and conclusions.

Its major contribution to your question is that paragraph order should follow the **logic needed by the reader to understand and accept the argument** rather than the chronological order in which the writer discovered the material.

That can imply sequences such as:

context → problem → claim → reasons → evidence → qualifications/objections → response → implication.

**APA citation**

Booth, W. C., Colomb, G. G., Williams, J. M., Bizup, J., & FitzGerald, W. T. (2016). *The craft of research* (4th ed.). University of Chicago Press. ISBN 978-0-226-23973-6.

---

## 13. Swales & Feak: select organization by communicative purpose and genre

This is especially valuable for academic writing because it explicitly treats **audience, purpose, strategy, organization, flow, general-to-specific and specific-to-general organization, problem–solution structures, processes, data commentary, critiques, and research-article sections**.

Swales and Feak's genre approach gives perhaps the clearest general rule for selecting organizational structure:

**Identify what communicative task the passage must perform, then use the rhetorical organization conventional for that task and discourse community.**

Thus:

- explaining a phenomenon may call for cause → effect;
- diagnosing something may call for problem → cause → solution;
- defining a concept often moves general → specific;
- synthesizing literature may organize around themes, controversies, methods, or chronology;
- establishing a research contribution can move from territory → gap → present research.

**APA citation**

Swales, J. M., & Feak, C. B. (2012). *Academic writing for graduate students: Essential tasks and skills* (3rd ed.). University of Michigan Press. https://doi.org/10.3998/mpub.2173936. ISBN 978-0-472-03475-8.

---

## 14. Foo & Lingard: explicit inter-paragraph coherence strategies

This recent article focuses specifically on **coherence between paragraphs**, rather than just within them. That makes it unusually well aligned with your question about sequencing paragraphs.

The authors' concept of “strategic paragraphing” treats paragraph transitions as substantive rhetorical relationships: writers should deliberately establish how a paragraph **extends, contrasts with, qualifies, explains, or otherwise develops** what precedes it.

**APA citation**

Foo, Y. Y., & Lingard, L. (2023). Strategic paragraphing 2.0: Techniques for enhancing inter-paragraph coherence. *Perspectives on Medical Education, 12*(1), 20–24. https://doi.org/10.5334/pme.727

A related concise discussion is:

Lingard, L. (2019). From semi-conscious to strategic paragraphing. *Perspectives on Medical Education, 8*(2), 98–100. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40037-019-0507-4

---

## 15. Siekmann et al.: evidence connecting structure/coherence with writing quality

This newer empirical study separates **text structure** from **coherence** and develops analytic criteria for both. It describes structure in terms of meaningful divisions such as introduction, body, and conclusion, while coherence concerns developing and connecting ideas through a common thread.

Its findings are particularly useful because both structure and coherence were associated with holistic assessments of writing quality.

**APA citation**

Siekmann, L., Parr, J. M., Busse, V., & McNamara, D. S. (2022). Structure and coherence as challenges in composition: A study of assessing less proficient EFL writers’ text quality. *Assessing Writing, 54*, 100672. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asw.2022.100672

---

## 16. Kobayashi: coherence may matter more than culturally preferred rhetorical pattern

Kobayashi's empirical study is a useful caution against assuming one culturally conventional organizational pattern is inherently superior. In an experiment involving 465 readers, culturally influenced rhetorical patterns affected judgments, but on a comparison/contrast task **paragraph-level coherence problems could outweigh rhetorical-pattern differences** in evaluations of writing quality.

The implication is important: the primary criterion should be whether readers can follow the relationships among ideas—not mechanical conformity to a single prescribed rhetorical pattern.

**APA citation**

Kobayashi, H. (1996). Factors affecting composition evaluation in an EFL context: Cultural rhetorical pattern and readers’ background. *Language Learning, 46*(3), 397–433. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1996.tb01242.x

# What this scholarship collectively suggests

Taken together, these sources support a fairly robust method for **choosing an organizational structure**, rather than starting with a canned outline.

## 1. Determine the rhetorical purpose first

Ask what the reader needs to understand, believe, compare, explain, decide, or do. Swales and Feak, Booth et al., and Mann and Thompson are especially useful here.

## 2. Identify the actual relationship among the ideas

Do the facts constitute a chronology, comparison, causal chain, classification, generalization/examples, problem/solution relationship, claim/evidence relationship, or some combination? Do not impose cause/effect on material that is really comparative, for example.

## 3. Prefer meaningful relational structures over arbitrary enumeration

Meyer and Freedle provide empirical support for this: comparison, causation, and problem/solution organization yielded better recall than merely presenting the same information as a collection of descriptions.

## 4. Make each paragraph perform a recognizable rhetorical function

A paragraph might establish a claim, provide evidence, explain a mechanism, present a counterargument, distinguish cases, supply an example, or derive an implication. Rodgers, RST, and McGee are particularly helpful for thinking this way.

## 5. Sequence paragraphs according to dependency

Put information earlier when later information depends upon it. Typical dependencies produce sequences such as:

> context → question → answer  
> problem → causes → possible solutions → evaluation  
> claim → evidence → interpretation → implication  
> phenomenon → explanation → consequences  
> general principle → specific cases → synthesis  
> existing view → complication → revised view

## 6. Manage given-to-new information flow

Connect each sentence and paragraph to information the reader already possesses, then advance toward new information. Kent and Weissberg are particularly relevant.

## 7. Use paragraph boundaries as rhetorical signals

A new paragraph should ordinarily tell the reader, “the discourse is moving to another meaningful unit”—not merely “this paragraph became long.” Experimental research confirms that paragraphing affects readers' processing of main versus subordinate information.

## 8. Judge the sequence by global coherence, not the number of transition words

Transitions help readers recognize relationships that already exist. They cannot repair an underlying sequence with no coherent conceptual relationship. Witte and Faigley and Fahnestock are foundational on this point.

## 9. Treat formulas such as PEEL/TEEL as scaffolds, not laws

Contemporary paragraph scholarship strongly cautions against equating effective paragraphing with one fixed template. McGee is especially explicit about the problems of rigid prescriptivism.

So, if I reduced the literature to one decision rule, it would be:

> **Choose the organizational structure that most accurately represents the logical or rhetorical relationships among the ideas, in the order that minimizes the reader’s need to infer those relationships.**

That is more defensible from rhetoric, discourse analysis, and comprehension research than rules such as “always put your strongest argument first,” “always use five paragraphs,” or “every paragraph must follow PEEL.”

# A compact core bibliography

If you want the **six sources most worth reading first**, I would start with Rodgers (1966) for paragraph theory; Fahnestock (1983) for paragraph coherence; Mann and Thompson (1988) for rhetorical relationships; Meyer and Freedle (1984) for experimental evidence about organizational structures; Williams and Bizup (2022) for highly usable coherence principles; and Swales and Feak (2012) for selecting structures according to academic genre and communicative purpose. These six together give you theory, empirical evidence, and practical application.
